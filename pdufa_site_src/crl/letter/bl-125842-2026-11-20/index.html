The structural blinding violation, granting simultaneous RTSM and EDC access to unblinded Applicant employees, is not remediated by assertions of non- exercise of the granted access by those employees.

Overall, BIMO inspection of Capricor, Inc. study records revealed a series of protocol and process violations in Study HOPE-3, creating critical violations of study conduct and compromising the reliability of clinical evidence from Study HOPE-3. The post inspection review of the investigative findings across the BIMO inspections provides independent, formal confirmation that Study HOPE-3 was not conducted in compliance with applicable Good Clinical Practice (GCP) requirements, consistent with ICH E9(R1).

2. The totality of evidence contained in the BLA does not provide substantial evidence of effectiveness of deramiocel in DMD

Despite the study conduct deficiencies and data integrity concerns, the FDA review team conducted a comprehensive review of all submitted data, including the additional data submitted in the major amendment and considered both Applicant- proposed indications. The FDA review team used Study HOPE-3 clinical protocol versions 1.0 to 7.0 and SAP versions 1.1 to 1.8, all created prior to Study HOPE-3 completion and data unblinding, for the analysis of the study results.

SAP version 3.0, the final SAP, was drafted after the randomized portion of Study HOPE-3 had concluded and in close proximity to the date of data unblinding. The Applicant's submitted analyses were represented as being based on SAP version 3.0; however, the analyses included in the Clinical Study Report (CSR) submitted in the BLA further deviated from SAP version 3.0.

The Applicant's analyses in the BLA included substantial modifications to the primary and secondary efficacy endpoint definitions and associated statistical analysis methods that drastically alter the study results in favor of deramiocel and those were implemented without reasonable scientific justification, appropriate documentation, and relevant safeguards including adherence to the study’s pre- specified blinding plan and SAP drafting plan.

Specifically, the study’s primary endpoint was changed from “change from baseline to month 12” to “percent change from baseline to month 12” in PUL 2.0 score after study HOPE-3 completion, a metric that has no corresponding clinical relevance and is, therefore, not interpretable. In addition, the intercurrent event handling in the Applicant’s analyses for the primary and secondary endpoints is based on imputing the worst observed value for one placebo subject whose withdrawal reason was recorded as due to an adverse event and removing improved scores for another placebo subject who had taken a prohibited medication. This imputation approach is not considered scientifically justified, as it unduly inflates the observed treatment effect, biases the results in favor of the deramiocel group, and lacks a reasonable scientific basis. This imputation resulted in this placebo subject artificially having the

largest decline in PUL 2.0 total score from baseline to month 12 among all randomized subjects (an 11.7-point decline from baseline).

In contrast, the FDA's independent analyses used all available data without imputation, consistent with the approach pre-specified in SAP versions 1.1 through 1.5, in order to avoid introducing bias. Therefore, FDA's analyses, rather than the Applicant's post-hoc analyses, form FDA’s basis for the evaluation of effectiveness. The following factors were considered in the FDA’s review:

a. Strength of the clinical evidence:

As described above, Study HOPE-3 did not meet its pre-specified primary and secondary efficacy endpoints, demonstrating no statistically significant differences between deramiocel and placebo at month 12 on PUL 2.0 total score. Based on FDA's analysis, the between-group difference in mean change from baseline in PUL 2.0 total score to month 12 was 0.66 (95% Cl: -0.45, 1.77; p=0.24) under SAP v1.1-v1.5, in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis set. This lack of statistical significance in the ITT population held across subsequent SAP versions: p=0.71 (v1.6), p=0.69 (v1.7), p=0.73 (v1.8), and p=0.16 (v2.0). Additional data from Study HOPE-3-OLE (the open-label extension of Study HOPE-3 in which all patients from Study HOPE-3 were treated with open-label deramiocel), submitted in the major amendment, demonstrated no difference in PUL 2.0 total score at month 24 between patients who received deramiocel for a full 24 months and those who were randomized to placebo over the first 12 months (Study HOPE-3) and only received deramiocel for the following 12 months. Similar findings were observed for LVEF and other clinical outcome measures. Notably, both groups also appear to have followed the known trajectory of decline in PUL 2.0 published in the literature in mixed (ambulatory/non-ambulatory) DMD patients over 12 and 24 months of observation (Mayhew et al., 2020).

b. Consistency and robustness of results across studies and across efficacy endpoints:

Study HOPE-2, the early-phase, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of deramiocel in a similarly mixed ambulatory/non-ambulatory DMD population (which was terminated prematurely after an interim data analysis), failed to demonstrate efficacy for its prespecified primary efficacy endpoint, change from baseline to month 12 in the mid-level (elbow) dimension of PUL version 1.2. Additionally, Study HOPE-2 failed to demonstrate efficacy on its prespecified secondary endpoints with no overall consistency in treatment effect across a large number of exploratory cardiac imaging endpoints. In addition to the premature study termination (with only one quarter of the intended sample size enrolled), there was a substantial amount of missing data, especially on

LVEF (15%), which may have introduced substantial selection bias into the study data.

In Study HOPE-3, PUL 2.0 total score, cardiac imaging parameter results, and results on other secondary endpoints, showed no statistically significant difference between deramiocel and placebo. Moreover, the methodological choices for data analysis in Study HOPE-3 drive the results, and the use of different intercurrent event handling strategies completely alter either the magnitude (for PUL 2.0 total score) or direction (for LVEF) of the results. Specifically, the Applicant’s analyses imputed the worst observed value for one placebo subject whose withdrawal was recorded as due to an adverse event and, removed the improved scores for another placebo subject who had taken prohibited medication — an approach, as previously described, that is not scientifically justified, unduly inflates the observed treatment effect, and biases the results in favor of the deramiocel group. The large degree to which these analytical choices determine the direction and magnitude of the study findings is itself evidence of the fragility and lack of robustness of those findings. The corresponding FDA analyses, which use all available data with no imputation to avoid introducing bias, do not show a statistically significant treatment effect. The sensitivity of the results to these analytical choices reflects a fundamental lack of robustness of the study HOPE-3 findings.

Across the clinical studies of deramiocel, five studies of intracoronary (IC) or central IV administration of deramiocel including four studies in adults with various cardiopulmonary conditions (myocardial infarction, pulmonary arterial hypertension, dilated cardiomyopathy, heart failure) and one study in males with DMD (Study HOPE-Duchenne) showed no evidence of a favorable treatment effect of deramiocel on cardiac outcomes. Furthermore, in Study HOPE- Duchenne, there was a higher incidence of cardiac adverse events in the deramiocel group compared to the standard-of-care group. Similarly, the results of Study HOPE-2 in patients with DMD, using IV deramiocel, showed no difference between deramiocel and the placebo group on upper limb function using PUL 1.2 mid-level or cardiac function (cardiac imaging parameters). Overall, there is lack of a consistent therapeutic effect across endpoints including cardiac and functional motor endpoints.

c. Specificity and biological plausibility of deramiocel’s therapeutic effect:

As a cellular product of cardiac origin (cardiosphere-derived cells), deramiocel was studied extensively for its cardiac-specific effects in clinical studies of cardiovascular conditions (myocardial infarction, pulmonary arterial hypertension, cardiomyopathy, heart failure) with no evidence of a favorable treatment effect on cardiac function. Furthermore, no efficacy of deramiocel on cardiac imaging parameters of function or structure has been demonstrated in the completed

studies in patients with DMD either using intracoronary administration (Study HOPE-Duchenne) or systemic administration (Study HOPE-2, Study HOPE-3).

In addition, deramiocel is proposed to exert anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects of paracrine nature based on in vitro pharmacology data using deramiocel and in vivo animal pharmacology data using a surrogate drug product. However, in vitro assays are inherently limited in their ability to model the complex biological interactions of the DMD microenvironment, and the in vivo studies were conducted with a surrogate product in a mouse model that does not fully recapitulate the human disease (DMD). Taken together, these nonclinical data are unable to establish a disease-relevant or disease-specific mechanism of action of deramiocel, and the functional relevance and translatability of the nonclinical findings to the clinical setting remain uncertain.

Overall, the totality of the submitted evidence does not provide substantial evidence of effectiveness of deramiocel for a proposed indication of treatment of cardiomyopathy or for treatment of upper limb impairment in patients with DMD and cannot support regulatory approval.

RECOMMENDATIONS FOR BLA RESUBMISSION

We recommend the following to support a future BLA resubmission:

Identify a (sub)population of patients with DMD with the highest likelihood of deriving clinical benefit from treatment with deramiocel based on mechanistic evidence and the exploratory analyses of clinical data from all completed studies in patients with DMD.

Propose clinically relevant, fit-for-purpose, clinical outcome measures that evaluate meaningful aspects of daily function in the selected, target population. For instance, in a non-ambulatory DMD population, use of PUL 2.0 (total score and/or subdomains) appears suitable to assess upper limb function that impacts daily life. In an ambulatory DMD population, measures of endurance and lower motor function such as North Star Ambulatory Assessment (NSAA) and/or timed motor tests may be considered.

Conduct a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical study in a homogeneous population of patients with DMD. Patients should be followed for at least 12-18 months from treatment initiation for assessment of the safety and effectiveness of deramiocel. We believe a randomized clinical study is both feasible and ethically permissible as deramiocel has not been established to be effective and, thus, clinical equipoise remains.

Consider whether higher dose(s) of deramiocel may be more optimal for efficacy and evaluate potential dose-response relationships with clinical outcome assessments in the selected population(s).

Implement close monitoring for serious hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, and of cardiac function through MR imaging (read centrally) including assessment of cardiac LV volumes over long-term follow up in patients treated with deramiocel.
